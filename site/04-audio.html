Audio: speech, music, sound effects and the mix

Audio carries more of a video's perceived quality than most engineers expect, and in explanatory videos the narration usually sets the timing for everything else. This page explains how each kind of sound is generated and how sound and picture are joined.

The four audio layers

Dialogue / narration

The information carrier. Text-to-speech (TTS), a cloned or designed voice, or a recorded human. Must be intelligible above everything else.

Music

Emotion and pacing. Generated, licensed from a library, or composed. Usually "ducked" (lowered) under speech.

Sound effects (SFX) & foley

Specific events: an engine ignition, a click, a whoosh on a transition. Generated, from libraries, or produced natively by a video model.

Ambience / room tone

Continuous background (wind, control-room hum) that makes cuts feel continuous and silence feel natural.

How text-to-speech works

Modern systems come in two broad families Verified (from the original papers):

  • Codec language models. A neural audio codec (SoundStream, EnCodec) compresses audio into discrete tokens. A transformer — essentially an LLM — predicts those tokens from the text plus a short voice sample. VALL-E showed zero-shot voice cloning from a 3-second prompt this way. Very natural prosody; can occasionally skip or repeat words, like any sampled LLM output.
  • Diffusion / flow-matching models. Generate spectrogram or latent features for the whole utterance at once (e.g. F5-TTS), then decode. Same family of ideas as image/video diffusion (page 2).

What you control: the text (punctuation is prosody), the voice, speed, and on newer models emotion/style tags or natural-language instructions ("calm, documentary tone"). SSML markup (pauses, emphasis, pronunciation) is supported by the big cloud speech services. Practice

Pronunciation is the classic TTS bug in technical videos

Acronyms, part numbers, units, and names ("Starship", "LOX", "T-minus", "Δv") are where TTS fails. An agent should keep a pronunciation lexicon for the project (phonetic respellings or SSML phonemes) and verify by running speech recognition on the output and diffing against the script. Inference

How music and sound effects are generated

Token language models

Music as codec tokens predicted by a transformer, conditioned on text (and optionally melody). Example: Meta's MusicGen. Verified

Latent diffusion with timing

A diffusion transformer over compressed audio latents, conditioned on text and on start time / total duration, so you can ask for "exactly 47 seconds". Example: Stable Audio. Stable Audio 3.0 (May 2026) released open-weight small/medium models trained on licensed data. Verified

Song generators with vocals

Commercial products generate full songs with lyrics. Architectures of the leading consumer products are not published. Unverified internals. Legal status matters — see below.

Video-to-audio (foley)

Models that watch a silent clip and generate synchronised sound (impacts, engines, footsteps), e.g. the open MMAudio. Useful for generated or rendered shots without sound. Practice

Status notes that affect tool choice (Oct 2026): the leading consumer song generator (Suno) has no official public API — "Suno APIs" sold online are unofficial wrappers; Udio disabled downloads in Oct 2025 after a label settlement; ElevenLabs and Google (Lyria) offer music generation through APIs. Details and dates: page 6 and page 12. Verified (vendor release notes and trade press, 2026-10-17)

Timing: how audio and picture are aligned

In narrated videos, the industry habit is audio-first: lock the narration, then cut picture to it. Practice For an agent this becomes a concrete data flow:

  1. Script → segments. Split the narration into segments that map to shots or beats (e.g. one paragraph per shot).
  2. Synthesise each segment separately (easy to regenerate one line later) or the whole script (more natural flow) — then measure each segment's duration.
  3. Get word timings. Either from the TTS vendor (ElevenLabs returns character-level alignment; Azure emits word-boundary and viseme events; Amazon Polly returns "speech marks") or by running forced alignment / ASR with word timestamps (WhisperX, faster-whisper) on the audio. Verified (vendor docs, 2026-10-17)
  4. Derive shot durations and cue points from the timings: shot 4 lasts as long as segment 4 plus a breath; the "landing legs deploy" animation starts at the word "legs".
  5. Generate/render picture to those durations, add music cut to the overall length, place SFX on the cue points.
  6. Build captions from the same word timings (no extra ASR needed when the script is known).

Try it: how long will my narration be?

Typical documentary narration runs at roughly 130–160 words per minute. Practice Paste a script to estimate duration and see why a "2-minute video" means about 260–320 words.

Lip-sync and visemes

For animated characters or avatars, mouths must match sounds. Two routes: (1) viseme events from TTS — Azure AI Speech emits 22 viseme IDs with timing, and 3D blend-shape frames (55 values at 60 fps) usable to drive a face rig Verified (Microsoft docs); (2) neural lip-sync models that modify video frames directly from audio (avatar platforms and open models, see page 6). Route 1 is exact and deterministic for rendered characters; route 2 is the only option for photoreal faces.

Loudness targets you will meet

EBU R128 (European broadcast): −23 LUFS integrated, max true peak −1 dBTP. ATSC A/85 (US broadcast): −24 LKFS. Verified (EBU). For online platforms, about −14 LUFS is the commonly used reference for YouTube and streaming, because louder uploads get turned down — but no official YouTube page states it. Unverified as an official spec; widely used Practice. LUFS and LKFS are the same ITU-R BS.1770 measurement.

What an agent can check in audio, objectively

  • Words: run ASR on the final mix and diff against the script (word error rate); catches TTS skips, mispronunciations that change words, and music masking speech. Practice
  • Levels: integrated loudness, true peak, loudness range; voice-to-music ratio in speech regions.
  • Timing: cue points vs animation event frames; gaps and overlaps between segments; total duration vs target.
  • Clipping, silence, DC offset, sample-rate mismatches: trivial to measure.
  • Hard to check automatically: whether the voice sounds natural and confident, whether music fits the mood, whether a sound effect feels cheap. Audio-capable multimodal models can give opinions, but treat them as a weak signal. Inference

Rights, consent and voices

Voice cloning of real people requires their consent; major vendors enforce verification for professional clones (ElevenLabs requires the voice owner to verify). Generated music has active litigation and plan-dependent commercial rights; many free tiers are non-commercial. Open TTS models have mixed licences (some MIT/Apache, some custom or non-commercial).
