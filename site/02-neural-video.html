Neural video generation: what happens inside the model

How a text prompt or an image becomes moving pixels, why it looks so good, and why it fails in the specific ways it does. No maths beyond what you need to reason about control and cost.

The intuition: sculpting a video out of noise

Imagine a block of TV static that is not just one frame but a whole stack of frames — a cube of noise with width, height and time. A video diffusion model has been trained on millions of real clips to answer one question: "given this noisy cube and this description, what would it look like with a little less noise?" Ask that question 20–50 times in a row and the static gradually resolves into a coherent clip that matches the description. Verified — this is the standard denoising-diffusion formulation (Ho et al., 2020).

Three engineering tricks make this practical for video:

  1. Work in a compressed space ("latent") instead of raw pixels — a video autoencoder (VAE) shrinks the clip first, the model denoises the small version, and the decoder blows it back up (latent diffusion, Rombach et al.).
  2. Use a transformer as the denoiser — the latent cube is cut into small "spacetime patches" that become tokens, like words in an LLM (Diffusion Transformer, Peebles & Xie; OpenAI's Sora technical report popularised the "spacetime patch" framing).
  3. Train with flow matching / rectified flow — a cleaner variant of diffusion where the model learns a velocity field that moves noise straight toward data, allowing fewer steps (Lipman et al., Liu et al.).

The pipeline, box by box

A concrete example of the numbers

The open Wan 2.1 report (arXiv 2503.20314) gives enough detail to make this tangible Verified:

  • The video VAE compresses 4× in time and 8×8 in space into 16 latent channels.
  • Latents are then "patchified" 2×2 in space, so a clip becomes (1 + T/4) × H/16 × W/16 tokens.
  • The prompt is encoded by the umT5 text encoder and injected through cross-attention; training uses rectified flow (flow matching).

So an 81-frame 1280×720 clip (≈ 5 s at 16 fps) becomes 21 × 45 × 80 ≈ 75,600 tokens, and full attention compares every token with every other token at every denoising step. Inference (arithmetic from the stated formula). Doubling the duration roughly doubles the tokens and roughly quadruples the attention cost. That one fact explains why clips are short, why long videos are made of stitched segments, and why 1080p/4K usually comes from an upscaling pass.

How motion is generated and kept consistent

There is no physics engine, skeleton or object list inside the model. Motion is a statistical pattern over the time axis of the latent cube, learned from real video. Two architectural families handle time differently:

Joint ("bidirectional") generation

All frames of the clip are denoised together with attention across time, so frame 1 "knows" about frame 80. Gives the most coherent short clips. Used by most current high-quality models. Cost grows fast with length.

Autoregressive / streaming generation

Frames (or chunks) are generated in order, each conditioned on the previous ones, like an LLM generating tokens. Enables long and real-time video and interactive "world models", but errors accumulate (drift). Research such as Self Forcing (2025) reduces the train/test gap and reports real-time streaming on a single GPU. Experimental

Temporal consistency within a clip comes from attention across frames. Consistency across clips does not exist unless you provide it: the next generation knows nothing about the previous one except what you pass in (reference images, the last frame, the same prompt wording). This is the central constraint an agent has to design around. Practice

Extending, editing and combining clips

  • Extension: generate a new segment conditioned on the last frames of the previous one. Each hop can drift; long "single takes" are usually several extensions with visible seams avoided by camera motion or cuts.
  • First/last-frame chaining: design keyframes as stills (cheap to check), then generate the motion between them. The most controllable way to tell a story with generated shots. Practice
  • Inpainting / object edit: regenerate only a masked region over time (remove a crane, change a logo).
  • Restyle / relight: video-to-video with a new prompt or reference.
  • Upscale & interpolate: separate models raise resolution and frame rate after generation (see page 5).
  • Combining: ordinary editing — cuts hide inconsistency better than long takes. Professional AI films are typically many short shots, heavily curated. Practice

Native audio: joint vs separate

Newer models can generate sound with the picture: either one model with an audio branch that attends to the video tokens, or a video model followed by a video-to-audio model. Joint generation gives tight sync for diegetic sound (footsteps, impacts, short dialogue). Separate production gives control: a consistent narrator, licensed music, stems you can mix. For narrated, edited videos the separate pipeline remains the norm; native audio is used for ambience or discarded. Inference (see page 4).

The control spectrum

What this means for an agent

  • Treat every generation as a sample: budget for several attempts per shot and a selection step. Practice
  • Move decisions left of the generator: produce and check keyframes, references and motion guides first; they are cheaper to inspect than video.
  • Keep anything that must be exactly right (labels, data, logos, physics) out of the generator and composite it afterwards.
  • Record model name, version, seed, prompt, references and parameters for every clip — providers update models and the same request may not reproduce later. Inference
  • Short shots, many cuts; length is a cost and quality multiplier.
